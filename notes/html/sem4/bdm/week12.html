Recap: The Slippery Word "Outlier"

This is the last lecture of the course, and it continues the topic of outliers. Two warnings from the previous lecture still hold, and everything below depends on them.

  • The word outlier is not well defined.
  • An outlier cannot be identified in isolation. You first collect some data, and only then say that some of the points in that sample are outliers. The outliers come from the same group of points as everyone else; a few of them simply stand apart.

Because of this, knowledge of the process (or the physics behind it) is essential. Only someone who understands how the data is produced can say what "abnormal" means.

Note for Review Whenever an outlier method looks too neat, ask: who decided what an outlier is? In both methods of this lecture the answer is "someone, from outside the algorithm", and that is the common thread of the whole chapter.

Two Roads to Outliers

In this lecture we look at outliers from the viewpoint of classification and clustering. Both make the situation even more confusing than before, because we now ask a machine-learning model to do the job of finding outliers.


Classification-based outlier detection means: take a classifier, and let it predict outliers. Any classifier will do.

The moment we say classification, we are saying that the data has labels (also called targets). That makes the whole approach supervised. The labels must also say whether a point is an outlier or not.

Clustering-based outlier detection works differently. It needs no labels, only a set of reference points. It is covered in its own section later.

Classification-Based Outlier Detection

The Chicken-and-Egg Problem

Take the usual image classification problem. You have thousands, maybe millions, of images, and suppose one particular image is an outlier.

The difficulty: how do we know it is an outlier before we have even started training the classifier?

This leads to a question about the training data: should the outliers be part of the training data or not? There are two scenarios.

Scenario 1 and Scenario 2

Scenario 1 Scenario 2
Training images Some images are known outliers Nobody knows or cares which are outliers
Labels Include an outlier label Only ordinary class labels l1​,l2​,l3​,l4​,…
Is it outlier detection? Yes, outlier is part of the problem No, it is plain image classification

In Scenario 2, you have 1 million images and you just want to train an image classifier and test how it performs. Some images may be outliers with respect to some reference, but you are not talking about outliers at all. So this scenario is of no help to us.

Therefore, classification-based outlier detection only makes sense in Scenario 1.

Outlier Becomes One of the Labels

Suppose the original labels are 0,1,2. Because of the way the images were captured, processed or stored, some of them turn out to be outliers. We add one more class for them. Call it label 3.

If an image is an outlier, label it 3. Label 3 means "outlier".

So the task becomes: train the classifier to predict several classes, and one of those classes (labels / targets) is "Outliers".

Somebody has already told the model "this image is an outlier". When we train, that becomes just another class. At deployment, the model may tell you "yes, this image looks like an outlier", because training images with that look carried the outlier label.

The Two-Class Limiting Case

The simplest version has only two classes:

Classes={normal, outlier}

A given test image is either a normal image or an outlier. You can also use more classes (0,1,2,…) with one of them marking outliers. Either way, the outlier is already defined in the labels.

Note for Review This is not true outlier discovery. Somebody already told the model what an outlier looks like, and the model is matching new images against that description. True discovery would mean: "here is some data, you tell me which points are outliers."

Two Things Outside the Algorithm's Control

  1. How outliers are defined. The classifier needs the data and the labels, and then predicts. It does not decide what counts as an outlier. That comes from outside, from the process or the application.
  2. Which classifier is used. It can be an SVM, a deep learning model or a simple linear classifier. The outlier definition does not rest on the choice.

The Imbalanced Data Problem

Classification-based outlier detection is an imbalanced (unbalanced) data problem. Outliers are few and far between by design, so the number of examples for the "outlier" label is very small compared with the others.

For example, with two classes:

Normal=1000,Outlier=52

Compare this with a usual classification set, where 1000 images over 4 classes would be roughly 250 per class. Here the classes are nowhere near equal. Another example: in 1000 training images, 929 may be normal and only 71 outliers.

So training a model to detect outliers is not straightforward. The practical challenges are:

  • The data is imbalanced (unequal samples per class).
  • The more fundamental issue remains: how did we decide those images were outliers? That question is not the business of classification-based outlier detection. We only say: give me the data and the labels, and I will predict the outliers.

Detected by Design: The Limitation

A classification algorithm detects only the outliers that have labels. This is what "by design" means here.

  • The method is supervised. You must tell it the label first. Only then can it say "this is an outlier".
  • It finds only the kinds of outlier that appear in the labels.
  • The reference is already provided by the labelled outliers.

What if a new kind of outlier shows up at test time, say a strange image that nothing like it was ever labelled as an outlier in training? The classifier will have difficulty calling it an outlier, because it was never trained for that. It works in a limited way: it catches outliers it was trained on, or ones similar (not identical) to them.

Note for Review Think of a security guard who has only seen photos of known shoplifters. He will spot them again, but a new kind of trouble walks right past him. Supervised detection = known troublemakers only.

Why Accuracy Lies

Accuracy is a simple idea:

Accuracy=total number of test pointsnumber of correct classifications​

and, if we talk about error:

Error=total number of test pointsnumber of misclassified points​

In this scenario, accuracy is not meaningful. Instead, we use precision and recall. The two steps are: the classifier first flags some points as possible outliers, and then some of the flagged points turn out to be real outliers.

Precision=#{flagged}#{flagged and outliers}​Recall=#{outliers}#{flagged and outliers}​

The numerator is the same in both: points that were flagged and were actually outliers (the correct catches). Only the denominator differs.

  • Precision asks: of everything I flagged, how much was really an outlier?
  • Recall asks: of all the real outliers, how many did I catch?

Precision and Recall Rescue the Day

The Fraud Example

We leave the image example and use 10,000 transactions, of which 100 are fraud. A fraud transaction is one done by somebody other than the authorised account holder (someone else spent the money). In our language, fraud = outlier. This gives a very clear definition of an outlier.

Outliers=100,Normal=10000−100=9900

We compare two detectors (classifiers).

Detector 1: Flags Nothing

  • Flags =0
  • Frauds caught (outliers correctly detected) =0

All 9,900 normal transactions are called normal correctly, and all 100 frauds are missed.

Accuracy=100009900​=0.99=99%Precision=00​⇒undefined (nothing flagged, so left blank)Recall=1000​=0

The detector does nothing about fraud, yet accuracy is 99%. The reason is the imbalance: only 1% of the data is outliers.

Detector 2: A Real Outlier Detector

  • Flags =150
  • Frauds caught =80

So among the 150 flagged transactions, 80 are real frauds and 150−80=70 are false alarms. Of the 100 real frauds, 100−80=20 were missed.

Flagged Not flagged Total
Actually fraud 80 20 100
Actually normal 70 9830 9900
Total 150 9850 10000

Here 9830=10000−80−20−70 are the normal transactions correctly left alone. The total number of mistakes is 70+20=90.

Comparing the Two

Detector Flags Frauds caught Accuracy Precision Recall
Flags nothing 0 0 99% — 0
Outlier detection (flag, detect) 150 80 99.1% 15080​≈0.53 10080​=0.8

(Precision of "Flags nothing" is undefined, so it has no bar.)

What this tells us:

  • Accuracy barely moves: 99% vs 99.1%.
  • Precision and recall are completely different: recall is 0 for the first detector and 0.8 for the second.
  • The second algorithm is working as expected. The first, despite its excellent accuracy, is not useful at all.

Note for Review Pro-Tip: This often appears on exams regarding imbalanced data. Remember that a model that always predicts "normal" gets accuracy =1−outlier fraction, which is very high when outliers are rare. That is why accuracy cannot be trusted here.

Precision and recall pull in opposite directions: flagging more points usually raises recall but lowers precision. If you want a single number combining them (not covered in the lecture), the F1 score is the harmonic mean:

F1​=P+R2⋅P⋅R​=0.533+0.82×0.533×0.8​≈0.64

Clustering-Based Outlier Detection

Clustering-based outlier detection has its own issues. Here is the story.

You have 10,000 data points. You apply a clustering algorithm, the simplest being K-means, and suppose you get 5 clusters. Each cluster has a cluster centre: C1​,C2​,C3​,C4​,C5​.

Now a new test point x arrives, and you want to know whether it is an outlier.

The Reference Assumption

When we clustered, we never worried about outliers inside those 10,000 points. They act as the reference points. So we must assume that the 10,000 reference points contain no outliers. In practice this is very difficult to guarantee, because when you collect data you may already have outliers in it, depending on the definition you use. But if we can treat these points as clean, the method works.

The Procedure

  1. Cluster the reference points (here K-means with K=5) and get the centres C1​,…,C5​.
  2. For the new point x, compute its score: the distance to every centre, then take the minimum.
s(x)=jmin​∥x−Cj​∥
  1. Apply a threshold (it comes from the application). If the score exceeds the threshold, x is an outlier.

Note for Review Distance here is usually Euclidean: ∥x−Cj​∥=∑i​(xi​−Cji​)2​. But it is only one choice. Any similarity measure or error measure can be used, chosen mostly by experiment for the application.

Worked Example

The distances from x to the five centres are:

Centre Distance ∣x−Cj​∣
C1​ 15.5
C2​ 29.5
C3​ 7.49
C4​ 2.94
C5​ 10.79

The minimum is at j=4:

s(x)=min{15.5, 29.5, 7.49, 2.94, 10.79}=2.94=∥x−C4​∥

So x is closest to cluster 4. But being closest is not enough. It must also be near that cluster, and "near" is defined by the threshold, which cannot be fixed beforehand (it depends on the application).

Suppose the threshold is 2. Then:

∥x−C4​∥=2.94>2

Since the minimum distance exceeds the threshold, x is declared an outlier. If it had been ≤2, x would not be an outlier.

In the picture, five clusters (each with a centre) sit around, and the new point x is measured against each centre. The shortest arrow is to C4​, but even that arrow is longer than the threshold allows.

The dashed red line is the threshold (2). Even the smallest bar (C4​, 2.94) sits above it, so x is an outlier.

The Full Pipeline

Assumptions to Keep in Mind

  • The 10,000 reference points contain no outliers.
  • The threshold must come from the application; it cannot be set a priori.
  • The "outlier" idea itself still has to be defined from the point of view of the process.

The Algorithm Does Not Matter

The same remark applies to both methods.

  • Classification side: SVM, deep learning, a linear classifier, a decision tree. The choice does not matter.
  • Clustering side: K-means, C-means (fuzzy), or any other clustering algorithm. It also does not matter.

What you finally apply is the idea explained above. You can even change the distance: any similarity measure or error measure may be defined, depending on the application and mostly chosen empirically.

The real concern in both cases is the same: an outlier must be defined properly from the viewpoint of the process itself. Once that is done, the rest falls into place.

Summary

Aspect Classification-based Clustering-based
Learning type Supervised Uses reference points, no labels
What is needed Labels, with one class = outlier Reference points assumed outlier free
How a point is judged Classifier predicts the outlier label s(x)=minj​∣x−Cj​∣ compared with a threshold
Main assumption Outliers are already labelled Reference data has no outliers
Main limitation Detects only labelled kinds of outliers Threshold depends on the application
Data issue Imbalanced classes (few outliers) Contaminated reference data
Right metrics Precision and recall, not accuracy Depends on the application
Algorithm choice SVM, deep learning, linear, tree: any K-means, C-means: any

Key ideas to carry away:

  • Define the outlier first; the algorithm comes second.
  • Classification-based detection treats "outlier" as just another class, so it is recognition, not discovery.
  • Accuracy hides the failure of a detector when outliers are rare; use precision and recall.
  • Clustering-based detection scores a new point by its distance to the nearest cluster centre and compares it with an application-driven threshold.

Practice Questions

  1. A bank has 10,000 transactions with 100 frauds. Detector A flags 400 transactions and 90 of them are real frauds. Detector B flags 100 transactions and 60 of them are real frauds. Work out the accuracy, precision and recall of both. Then argue which one you would deploy if every missed fraud costs the bank far more than an investigated false alarm, and what would change in your answer if investigators can only review 100 flags a day.

  2. A factory trains an image classifier on photos from camera A, with label 3 meaning "defective product". Months later, camera B is installed and a completely new kind of defect (a glare pattern never seen before) appears. Explain what the classifier is likely to do with these images and why. Then describe two changes to the training process or data you would consider, and what each one cannot fix.

  3. You build a clustering-based detector with K=5 on 10,000 reference points, but it later turns out that 3% of those points were outliers that happened to be very similar to each other. What does this do to the clusters and centres? How would a new test point that looks like those hidden outliers be judged, and why does this show that the method is only as good as its reference data?

  4. In the example, the minimum distance 2.94 was compared with a single threshold of 2. Suppose cluster 4 is very tight, while cluster 2 is very spread out. Would one global threshold treat a test point near cluster 4 and a test point near cluster 2 fairly? Propose how you could choose thresholds differently and what extra information you would need.

  5. A training set has 929 normal images and 71 outlier images. A lazy classifier predicts "normal" for everything. Calculate its accuracy, precision and recall. Then you duplicate the outlier images until both classes are equal and retrain. Predict, with reasoning, what happens to the recall and precision on a realistic test set where outliers are still rare, and why the improvement in one may come at the cost of the other.

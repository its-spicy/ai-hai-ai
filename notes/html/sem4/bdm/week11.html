1. The Story So Far

A video-streaming service records what each viewer watches in every session. Five pieces of content exist: A,B,C,D,E. Each session is a transaction, and the content watched in it is the itemset of that transaction. Nothing else is recorded: no ratings, no genres, no target label. This is the same problem that was started in the previous lecture, and this lecture finishes it.

The transaction database (10 sessions):

Transaction T1​ T2​ T3​ T4​ T5​ T6​ T7​ T8​ T9​ T10​
Itemset A, B, C A, B A, C B, D A, B, C B, D, E A, C A, B B, C, D A, B

Minimum support: minsup=0.4=40%

What was already done (recap of the Apriori run)

  • Step 0 (search space): with 5 items there are 25−1=31 possible non-empty itemsets.
  • Step 1 (1-itemsets): evaluate {A},{B},{C},{D},{E}. D and E are infrequent, so they are discarded. {A,B,C} remain.
  • Step 2 (2-itemsets): form {AB,AC,BC} from the surviving items. BC is infrequent (circled and struck out in the working), leaving {AB,AC}.
  • Why we stop: BC⊆{A,B,C}, and BC is infrequent, so {A,B,C} is infrequent too. Any 4-itemset (such as ABCD) and the 5-itemset ABCDE also contain an infrequent subset, so they are infrequent as well and never need to be examined.

Final frequent itemsets:

{A, B, C, AB, AC}

Out of 31 possible itemsets, only these 5 move forward. That is the whole payoff of the Apriori property, and the reduction is systematic, not luck.

Note for Review Where does "about 74% reduction" come from? Only 8 itemsets were ever counted (5 singles + 3 pairs), so 31−8=23 were never evaluated, and 3123​≈74.2%. If you instead count how many of the 31 failed to survive, you get 3126​≈83.9%. Both are correct statements about different things, so say which one you mean.

3. Pruning with the Apriori Property

The property. If an itemset is frequent, every one of its subsets is frequent. Equivalently, if Y⊆X then

Sup(Y)≥Sup(X)

Flip it around (the contrapositive) and you get the weapon we actually use: if a subset is infrequent, every superset is infrequent, so do not even count it.

Checking it on our data: BC has support 0.3, and its superset ABC has support 0.2 (found in T1​,T5​ only). We never needed to count ABC to know it fails.

What got pruned, and why

Pruned group Reason
Every set containing D or E (22 sets) D and E are infrequent
ABC (1 set) Superset of infrequent BC

Counting it up: 7 pairs + 10 triples + 5 quadruples + 1 quintuple =23 itemsets never evaluated.

Green = frequent, red = infrequent, grey = pruned without ever being counted.

Why this matters: the search space explodes

For n items the number of non-empty itemsets is

2n−1

Even on a log axis, the curve is a straight climb: every extra item doubles the search space. Our n=5 gives 31. A modest catalogue is another story (see section 8).

4. Frequent Itemsets to Association Rules

Now that the frequent itemsets are fixed, we turn them into rules. An association rule is written

X→YmeaningP(Y∣X)
  • X is the antecedent (what was watched first).
  • Y is the consequent (what we predict will follow).
  • The rule is conditional and directional: it reads "given X, how likely is Y?"

The golden constraint

X∩Y=∅

The antecedent and the consequent must share no items. The reason is physical: if a viewer watched X, we want to know whether they went on to watch Y. If X also sits inside Y, we are "predicting" something we already know, and we learn nothing new.

Examples of invalid rules (they share an item):

  • A→AC (the A appears on both sides)
  • AB→BC (the B appears on both sides)

You can write these down, but they make no practical sense, so we never generate them.

Generating the rules from our frequent itemsets

Frequent itemsets: {A,B,C,AB,AC}

  • Single-item sets A, B, C: nothing to associate. Association needs at least two items, so no rules come from these.
  • AB gives two directions: A→B and B→A.
  • AC gives two directions: A→C and C→A.
  • BC is not here (infrequent), so it produces nothing.

Result: 4 directional rules.

Each 2-itemset {X,Y} produces exactly two rules, one per direction. The two directions are generally not equivalent, which is why the order of the arrow matters in everything that follows.

Note for Review Pro-Tip: Examiners love asking "how many rules come from this itemset?" For a k-item frequent itemset, every non-empty proper subset can act as an antecedent, giving 2k−2 rules. For k=2 that is 2; for k=3 it is 6. Remember: the consequent is simply whatever is left over, so the constraint X∩Y=∅ is automatically satisfied.

5. Confidence: How Often Does the Rule Hold?

Conf(X→Y)=Sup(X)Sup(X∪Y)​

In words: of all the sessions containing X, what fraction also contain Y? It is just P(Y∣X).

Calculations, one rule at a time

A→B

Conf(A→B)=Sup(A)Sup(AB)​=0.70.5​=75​=71.4%

B→A

Conf(B→A)=Sup(B)Sup(AB)​=0.80.5​=85​=62.5%

A→C

Conf(A→C)=Sup(A)Sup(AC)​=0.70.4​=74​=57.14%

C→A

Conf(C→A)=Sup(C)Sup(AC)​=0.50.4​=54​=80%
Rule Calculation Confidence Interpretation
A→B 5/7 71.4% Among sessions with A, 71.4% also contained B
B→A 5/8 62.5% Among sessions with B, 62.5% also contained A
A→C 4/7 57.14% Among sessions with A, 57.14% also contained C
C→A 4/5 80% Among sessions with C, 80% also contained A

Direction matters. A→B (71.4%) and B→A (62.5%) are different numbers with different meanings, because the denominator changes (the support of the antecedent). The direction always runs from antecedent to consequent.

The weakness of confidence

Confidence is built purely from how often things occurred. If an item is simply very popular, its confidence as a consequent will be high no matter what. This is the problem lift is designed to fix.

6. Lift: Is It Real or Just Popular?

Lift divides confidence by how common the consequent already is on its own (its baseline):

Lift(X→Y)=Sup(Y)Conf(X→Y)​=Sup(X)Sup(Y)Sup(X∪Y)​

Because it is normalized against the baseline, it gives a fair reading:

Lift value Meaning
>1 Positive association: Y appears more often after X than usual
=1 Neutral: X tells us nothing about Y
<1 Negative association: Y appears less often after X than usual

Worked example from the lecture

Lift(C→A)=0.70.8​=1.143

Here 0.8 is Conf(C→A) and 0.7 is Sup(A).

All four rules, with every step

A→B: confidence 0.714, baseline Sup(B)=0.8

Lift(A→B)=0.80.714​=0.893

Cross-check with the support form:

Sup(A)Sup(B)Sup(AB)​=0.7×0.80.5​=0.560.5​=0.893

B→A: confidence 0.625, baseline Sup(A)=0.7

Lift(B→A)=0.70.625​=0.893

A→C: confidence 0.5714, baseline Sup(C)=0.5

Lift(A→C)=0.50.5714​=1.143

Cross-check:

0.7×0.50.4​=0.350.4​=1.143

C→A: confidence 0.8, baseline Sup(A)=0.7

Lift(C→A)=0.70.8​=1.143

Complete rule table

Rule Support Confidence Lift Interpretation
A→B 50% 71.4% 0.893 B is already common (80% baseline); 71.4% is below that baseline
B→A 50% 62.5% 0.893 A is already common (70% baseline); 62.5% is below that baseline
A→C 40% 57.14% 1.143 C occurs more often with A than it does in general
C→A 40% 80% 1.143 A occurs more commonly with C than it does in general

For the rules with lift below 1, the weaker evidence means we cannot say that watching one leads to watching the other. Notice that A→B has a decent-looking 71.4% confidence yet a lift of only 0.893. That is exactly the trap: B shows up in 80% of all sessions, so 71.4% is actually worse than guessing.

How to read the chart: wherever the purple bar (confidence) sits above the orange bar (baseline), lift is above 1 (A→C and C→A). Where purple sits below orange, lift is below 1 (A→B and B→A).

Note for Review Pro-Tip: Lift is symmetric: Lift(X→Y)=Lift(Y→X), because the support form Sup(X)Sup(Y)Sup(X∪Y)​ does not care about the order. You can see it in the table: both A↔B rules have 0.893 and both A↔C rules have 1.143. Confidence is not symmetric. So lift tells you whether two items go together; confidence tells you how reliable one direction is.

7. Practical Interpretation: Who Gets Recommended What

Now suppose a new viewer arrives (not from our transaction database), and we watch what they play.

Case 1: the new session contains C.

  • Relevant rule: C→A
  • P(A∣C)=80% and Lift=1.143, a positive association.
  • Decision: recommend A. Past viewers who watched C very often liked A as well.

Case 2: the new session contains B.

  • Relevant rule: B→A
  • Lift=0.893<1.
  • Decision: do not recommend A on the strength of B. Past viewers did not particularly follow B with A, so the association is weak.

The same logic applies if the items were products: someone buying C gets A suggested immediately, while someone buying B does not, because the lift says the link is smaller than chance. All of this rests on historical data, so more and better data means better recommendations.

8. Apriori-Based ARM Is a Basic Recommender System

Put everything together (frequent itemsets via Apriori pruning, then rules, then confidence and lift, then recommendations) and you have a basic recommender system. It is "basic" because it is based only on the numbers in the transaction data. A richer system would also use genre, age group, and other context.

Why it is called objective

Every term has been defined precisely, so the method is completely objective and reproducible: give it a transaction database, follow the Apriori property, prune, generate rules, compute confidence and lift, and you will always arrive at the same recommendations.

Why the pruning step is non-negotiable

Take a streaming service with just 100 pieces of content. The number of possible itemsets is

2100−1≈1.27×1030

and the number of association rules grows even faster. Real services have far more than 100 titles, so brute force is impossible. The Apriori pruning is the step that keeps the whole thing computationally manageable, and it must be considered whenever you design or implement a recommender.

Two things that go hand in hand

  1. The data itself gives you the objective recommender described above.
  2. Studying the transaction data further (extra attributes, context, patterns) lets you improve the recommender beyond the basic version.

10. Unsupervised, Not Supervised

This is very different from supervised machine learning:

  • There is no target variable. All we are given is what happened in a session: one viewer watched A,B,C; another watched D,E; another watched only A; another watched A,D, and so on.
  • Data capture is minimal. We simply record what is watched, session by session, because we only want to establish associations.
  • After that, the steps above give the final recommendation system. Because there is no target, this is unsupervised learning.

Some ideas from supervised ML can be borrowed if a target is explicitly specified (for example, if you want to optimize for a particular goal), but that is a special case. What we studied is the general, basic version.

Note for Review Pro-Tip: A classic exam contrast. Supervised: labelled rows, a target column (like the Stay/Leave decision in a classification tree). Association rule mining: unlabelled transactions, no target, and the output is a set of rules rather than a predicted label.

12. Think-It-Through Questions

These are not plug-in-the-formula questions. Work from the 10-session streaming database of this lecture unless told otherwise.

  1. The popularity trap. Suppose the service adds 100 new sessions in which only B was watched (nothing else), giving 110 sessions in total. Before computing anything, predict qualitatively what happens to Conf(A→B), Conf(B→A) and Lift(A→B). Then compute all three and check your prediction. What does this tell you about trusting confidence alone?

  2. Same lift, different action. A viewer has just watched A and nothing else. The rules A→B (confidence 71.4%, lift 0.893) and A→C (confidence 57.14%, lift 1.143) both apply. A product manager insists on "always recommend the rule with the highest confidence". Which item would that policy suggest, which item would a lift-based policy suggest, and which would you defend in the meeting? Use the baseline supports in your argument.

  3. Counting the savings at scale. A catalogue has 100 titles. After the single-item pass, only 30 titles are frequent. How many evaluations did levels 1 and 2 require with Apriori pruning, and how many would they have required without it? Why is it impossible to compute the number of 3-itemset candidates from the figure "30 frequent items" alone? What extra information would you need?

  4. Moving the threshold. Lower the minimum support to 30%, treating "support ≥30%" as frequent. Redo the Apriori run: list every frequent itemset, state how many evaluations you needed, and say whether ABC is still pruned and why. Which new rules appear, and do any of them have a lift above 1?

  5. Two items in the antecedent. A new viewer's session already contains both A and C. None of our four rules has {A,C} as an antecedent. Explain why the rule AC→B is valid in form, why it could not be generated at 40% minimum support, and compute its confidence and lift directly from the transactions. Would you recommend B to this viewer? Justify with your numbers.
